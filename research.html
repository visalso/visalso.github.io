Scalable neutral-atom quantum computing

In the Endres Lab at Caltech, I work on developing scalable quantum processors based on arrays of individually trapped and controlled neutral atoms. In these systems, atoms confined in optical tweezers serve as qubits, while strong interactions between highly excited Rydberg states enable entangling operations. The flexibility and reconfigurability of neutral-atom arrays make them a promising platform for realizing large-scale quantum computers.

My research focuses on simplifying and improving neutral-atom quantum computing architectures to enable efficient and reliable control of increasingly large qubit arrays. As these systems scale, maintaining high-fidelity quantum operations while managing the complexity of individual-qubit addressing, coherent control, and experimental hardware becomes a significant challenge. Addressing these limitations is essential for translating the capabilities of neutral-atom platforms into practical quantum computing systems.

A central question driving my work is: How can we scale neutral-atom quantum processors to ten thousands of qubits and beyond while preserving fast, high-fidelity individual-qubit control and enabling reliable quantum operations?

Ultimately, my goal is to help bridge the gap between state-of-the-art neutral-atom experiments and practical, large-scale quantum computation by developing more scalable, efficient, and robust approaches to quantum processor design and control.

Programmable chemical dynamics with trapped ions

Charge and excitation transfer are fundamental processes in physical, chemical, and biological systems, ranging from redox reactions to photosynthetic light harvesting. In condensed-phase environments, these processes are governed by the interplay between coherent electronic and vibrational interactions and the dissipation induced by the surrounding environment. Understanding these complex quantum dynamics remains challenging, both computationally and experimentally, particularly in regimes where electronic, vibrational, and environmental energy scales become comparable.

A central question motivating my research is: How do coherent interactions, molecular vibrations, and environmental dissipation collectively influence quantum excitation-transfer dynamics?

During my doctoral research in Guido Pagano's Quantum Simulation Lab at Rice University, I explored this question using trapped-ion quantum simulators to realize chemically motivated models of open quantum systems. By exploiting the independent light-matter interactions of dual-species ion chains, we engineered coherent spin-phonon couplings and controllable dissipation through sympathetic cooling. This approach enables precise and independent control over electronic, vibrational, and environmental parameters, allowing us to probe real-time quantum dynamics across a wide range of interaction regimes.

Our research has led to two complementary experimental studies:

Electron transfer with tunable dissipation

In V. So et al., Science Advances 10, eads8011 (2024) (Publication ↗), we experimentally simulated a donor-acceptor electron-transfer model coupled to a damped collective vibrational mode. Using a dual-species trapped-ion system, we independently controlled the donor-acceptor energy gap, electronic and vibronic coupling strengths, and environmental relaxation rate. By directly observing the time-resolved population dynamics, we characterized electron transfer across different regimes of adiabaticity and dissipation. In particular, we identified optimal transfer conditions arising from the competition between coherent electronic coupling and dissipative relaxation, revealing how the environment can facilitate efficient transfer in open quantum systems.

Multimode charge and exciton transfer

In V. So et al., Nature Communications 17, 438 (2026) (Publication ↗), we extended this approach to a dissipative two-mode linear vibronic-coupling model, investigating both charge transfer and vibrationally assisted exciton transfer. By independently engineering the vibrational frequencies, coupling strengths, and dissipation rates, we explored how multiple vibrational modes reshape the underlying transfer dynamics. We found that degenerate vibrational modes can enhance transfer rates at large donor-acceptor energy gaps, while nondegenerate modes introduce additional transfer pathways that reduce sensitivity to the energy mismatch. These results reveal how multimode vibronic interactions and interference effects can significantly modify quantum transport beyond the predictions of simpler single-mode models.

Both studies were conducted in close collaboration with theorists in chemical physics and biophysics, demonstrating a scalable, hardware-efficient approach to simulating chemically relevant quantum dynamics with engineered environments. Together, they establish a foundation for investigating increasingly complex vibronic systems and provide insights into the microscopic mechanisms governing charge and energy transfer in molecular and biological systems.

Quantum reservoir engineering with trapped ions

Quantum simulation relies not only on the precise control of coherent interactions but also on the ability to engineer and manipulate the surrounding environment. In many physical, chemical, and biological systems, thermal fluctuations and dissipation play essential roles in shaping quantum dynamics. However, reproducing these effects in a controlled laboratory setting remains a significant experimental challenge. As trapped-ion quantum simulators increasingly exploit collective motional modes to represent bosonic degrees of freedom, developing robust methods for thermal-state preparation and programmable dissipation becomes particularly important.

A central question motivating my research is: How can we combine established experimental techniques in new ways to engineer programmable quantum environments and expand the capabilities of trapped-ion quantum simulators?

My research explores how existing experimental tools can be integrated to realize new forms of quantum control without substantially increasing hardware complexity. In particular, I focus on engineering bosonic reservoirs with independently tunable effective temperatures and dissipation rates, enabling the study of open quantum systems under controlled finite-temperature conditions.

Engineering finite-temperature bosonic reservoirs

In V. So et al., Physical Review Letters 137, 050604 (2026) (Publication ↗), we developed and experimentally demonstrated a versatile reservoir-engineering technique by combining two well-established tools: controlled motional heating and continuous resolved-sideband cooling. By injecting stochastic electric-field signals through an external antenna while simultaneously applying laser cooling, we engineer a competition between phonon excitation and relaxation. This approach enables independent control of the effective reservoir temperature and dissipation rate, allowing selected vibrational modes of trapped ions to evolve toward well-defined thermal states.

We benchmarked this technique by investigating finite-temperature effects in two chemically motivated quantum simulation models. In a donor-acceptor electron-transfer system, we observed that increasing the reservoir temperature broadens the transfer-rate spectrum, suppressing transfer at small energy gaps while enhancing it at larger gaps. We further extended our approach to a two-mode vibrationally assisted exciton-transfer model, demonstrating how local temperature can activate additional quantum interference pathways. These experiments highlight the importance of accurately incorporating thermal environments when investigating nonequilibrium quantum dynamics.

Beyond these applications, our method provides a flexible and hardware-efficient framework for dissipative thermal-state preparation and the simulation of bosonic and spin-boson models. More broadly, this work illustrates how creatively combining established experimental techniques can unlock new capabilities in quantum hardware, expanding the range of physical phenomena accessible to programmable quantum simulators.
